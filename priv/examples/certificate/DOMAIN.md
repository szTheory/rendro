# Certificate — Domain Anatomy

Domain-research notes for the certificate example family. This document captures
the language, readers, reading situations, and layout conventions that a faithful
certificate must honor. It is cited by rubric-scored demos (SHOW-01) so that
scoring is anchored to how certificates are actually read and used — not to
generic document-formatting taste.

## Domain Language

The vocabulary a reader expects a certificate to speak. Missing or renamed terms
make a document read as "not really a certificate."

**Nouns (the things on the page)**

- **Certificate** — the document itself; a formal attestation that a named person
  earned, completed, or was awarded something. Its value is ceremonial and evidentiary.
- **Title** — what the certificate certifies, e.g. "Certificate of Completion" or
  "Certificate of Achievement."
- **Recipient** — the named person the certificate is about. The anchor of the document.
- **Issuing body** — the institution or program conferring the credential and standing
  behind it.
- **Body statement** — the sentence describing what the recipient did to earn it.
- **Date** — when the credential was conferred.
- **Seal / signature line** — the mark of authority: a signatory's name and role, and
  optionally a seal or emblem, that makes the certificate credible and countable.
- **Credential** — the achievement or status being conferred (a course, an award, a
  compliance qualification).

**Verbs and events (the lifecycle)**

- **Award / confer** — the issuing body grants the credential to the recipient.
- **Complete / earn** — the recipient does the work that qualifies them.
- **Sign / seal** — an authorized signatory validates the certificate.
- **Present / display** — the recipient shows or frames the certificate as proof.
- **Verify** — a third party confirms the certificate is genuine and current.

## Personas & Jobs-to-be-Done

Who reads a certificate, why, and — critically — the ONE fact each reader needs first.

- **Recipient — primary reader.** Receives the certificate as recognition and keeps or
  displays it. Their job is: confirm this is mine and it says what I earned. The ONE
  fact they need first is their **own name (the recipient)**, immediately followed by
  the **credential** it attests. The document is read as a moment of pride, not a queue.

- **Verifier / evaluator — secondary reader.** A hiring manager, registrar, or auditor
  checking the credential is real. Their job is: confirm who earned what, from whom, and
  when. They need the **recipient**, the **issuing body**, and the **date** to be
  unambiguous, plus a **signature/seal** that signals authority.

- **Presenter / awarding official — tertiary reader.** Reviews the certificate before it
  is conferred. Their job is: confirm the certificate is correct and dignified enough to
  hand over. They check spelling of the recipient's name, the title, and that the
  document looks formal and credible.

## Reading Context

The situations in which certificates are actually read — which drive what must survive.

- Certificates are **read ceremonially and kept, not triaged**: the first read is a
  slow, deliberate one — a moment of recognition rather than a scan in a queue.
- Certificates are **printed, framed, and displayed at a distance**, so the recipient's
  name and the title must carry across a room, not just at reading distance.
- A **verification read** happens later and out of context, when a stranger checks the
  credential, so the recipient, issuer, and date must be self-evident without any
  supporting story.
- Certificates are **often landscape and formal by convention**, echoing diplomas and
  awards; the format itself signals gravity, so the layout must read as dignified.

## Layout & Typographic Conventions

The visual grammar that makes a document read as a trustworthy certificate.

- **The recipient's name is the single most visually prominent element** — the largest,
  most central text, so the certificate is unmistakably about that person.
- **The title sits prominently above or near the recipient**, naming what is certified
  before the eye reaches the body statement.
- **The composition is centered and symmetrical**, the formal convention that
  distinguishes a certificate from a business form and signals ceremony.
- **The body statement is set in a calm, readable block** beneath the name, describing
  the credential without competing with the recipient for prominence.
- **The signature / seal line anchors the lower portion**, giving the document its mark
  of authority and closing the composition.
- **Generous margins and an optional keyline frame** give the certificate the formal,
  bounded, display-worthy feel expected of an award — never a cramped business layout.
